The receipt GDP doesn't print.

Renato Vargas

Economist · Environmental Accounts Expert

I help statistical offices, central banks, and development agencies build the accounts that tie an economy to the land, water and air it runs on.

Everything an economy produces draws on nature twice: directly, in the activity that makes it, and indirectly, through every supplier in its value chain. The second part never shows on the price tag.

This island makes it visible. It is a small economy modelled on a real one. Click any district to spend 100 million there and read what the bill prints: where the money travels, and the jobs, energy, water and emissions used along the way.

    FIG. 1 — Nature Island: seven districts, the trade between them, and the accounts kept on each. The arithmetic underneath is an environmentally-extended input-output model. Spending in one district is traced to the district's own suppliers, then to their suppliers, and so on down the chain; the jobs, energy, water and emissions each of them uses are added up, and the total is what the receipt prints. Modelled on a real economy; names invented, figures rounded.

    Eight questions I get hired to answer.

    The island runs on one of them. Each method below is listed by the question it settles, the instruments it takes, and where it has already been put to work.

    § 1

    Environmental-economic accounts

    How much water, energy, forest and waste sits behind each unit of GDP?

    With
    SEEA Central Framework, supply and use tables, energy, emissions, water and forest accounts
    Seen in
    Guatemala, Kyrgyz Republic, Eastern and Southern Africa
    § 2

    Bioeconomy satellite accounts

    What share of the economy is biological in origin, and who depends on it?

    With
    National accounts reclassified product by product, in R, so the result can be rebuilt every year
    Seen in
    Costa Rica, Ecuador, Colombia, and a 13-country study for Latin America and the Caribbean
    § 3

    Environmentally-extended input-output

    If one sector grows, who else gets busy, and what does it burn?

    With
    Input-output tables, multipliers, regionalization, satellite extensions for emissions, energy and waste
    Seen in
    Tourism in Fiji, waste in Egypt, mining in Brazil, Chile, Peru and South Africa
    § 4

    General equilibrium modelling

    When a policy moves one price, what happens to everything else?

    With
    GTAP, IEEM and the PEP family of models; social accounting matrices; GAMS
    Seen in
    Forests and fuelwood in Guatemala, decarbonization in Costa Rica, land use in Colombia
    § 5

    Household surveys and poverty

    Who pays for the energy transition, and who can't?

    With
    Household budget and living-standards surveys, welfare and distributional analysis in R and Stata
    Seen in
    Climate and development reports for Georgia, Armenia and Azerbaijan; Dominican Republic
    § 6

    Ecosystem services and land use

    Where on the map does the risk fall, and what is standing there?

    With
    InVEST, land-use change models, geographic information systems, night lights and elevation data
    Seen in
